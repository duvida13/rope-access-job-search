# Vacancy hunt fails, tax math survives

The headline finding of this research round is a **failure, not a discovery**: the goal of collecting 30-50 real, currently-open offshore/rope-access vacancies across the UK, Netherlands, Norway and Denmark — each priced with day/hour/month rate, rotation, paid hours, overtime, accommodation/travel and contractor-vs-employee status — was **not achieved**. Four parallel research agents hit a single Indeed API quota that turned out to be rate-limited **at the account level, shared across all concurrent callers**, not per-agent as assumed; running four agents simultaneously exhausted it almost immediately, and even this coordinating session's own solo retries over several minutes never saw the cooldown timer count down to zero — it kept climbing back up, meaning something else outside this session's control kept consuming the same quota throughout. The combined result across all four countries is **essentially zero vacancy-level pay, rotation, accommodation or employment-status data** — the UK returned nothing usable at all despite 20+ retries; the Netherlands returned 3 raw search hits but zero successful detail lookups, and 2 of those 3 were irrelevant warehouse-manager postings; Norway returned 9 real, relevant job titles but, again, zero successful detail lookups; Denmark returned nothing from Indeed and only 2-3 unverifiable named leads from web search, with career-site pages blocked by network egress. What this round did produce, well, is current 2026 income-tax and social-security facts for all four countries — enough to convert an already-known gross salary into an estimated net figure, though not the fresh, vacancy-grounded comparison the assignment actually asked for.

## Zero for four on the actual target

None of the four country agents cleared the bar of "a real, individually verified vacancy with pay and rotation data." The table below is the honest scorecard.

| Country | Raw hits via Indeed | Usable detail (pay/rotation/description) | Relevant to rope access/offshore | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| UK | 0 | 0 | — | 20+ retries over 15+ minutes, all rate-limited; one unverifiable AI-summary lead (Oceaneering, Aberdeen) |
| Netherlands | 3 | 0 | 1 of 3 (Equans "Operator E&I Offshore 2/2", title only) | 2 of 3 hits were Bilfinger warehouse-manager postings, not rope access |
| Norway | 9 | 0 | 9 of 9 (title/company/location/date only) | The best haul of the round — real, relevant, but no `get_job_details` succeeded for any of them |
| Denmark | 0 | 0 | 2-3 leads (Vestas Esbjerg, Global Wind Service Fredericia, iPS Baltic) | Leads came from web search, not Indeed; career pages blocked by egress proxy, so none could be confirmed against actual posting text |

Every single one of these shortfalls traces to the same root cause, confirmed independently by all four country notes: `mcp__Indeed__search_jobs` and `mcp__Indeed__get_job_details` calls failed with "Rate limit exceeded for account 231207884 on toolset claude," and the failure did not resolve with patience — waits of 30 to 150 seconds between retries, repeated 15-35 times per country over 15-20 minutes, never worked, and the reported cooldown timer moved in the wrong direction. That is the signature of a shared quota under concurrent contention, not a per-session cap that clears with a short pause — and running four country agents in parallel against exactly that kind of quota was the proximate mistake this round made. A secondary, compounding problem was that the network egress proxy in this environment blocked WebFetch to essentially every career site tried as a fallback — gov.uk, careers.vestas.com, globalwindservice.recruitee.com, bluestreamoffshore.com, sky-access.com, ropeaccessnoord.nl, fenderbv.nl, bilfinger.com, skatteetaten.no, belastingdienst.nl — so even the fallback plan of manually checking named employers' own postings was unavailable this session.

## The tax facts, by contrast, came through solidly — with two open disputes

Each country note delivered a workable set of 2026 tax and social-security facts for a single filer with no dependents, well-sourced enough to build a net-pay estimate on, though none were cross-checked against the primary government tax authority site (gov.uk, skatteetaten.no, belastingdienst.nl and skat.dk were all blocked from direct fetch this session, so every figure below traces to secondary tax-guide aggregators, not primary legislation).

**UK**: Personal Allowance **£12,570** (tax-free, tapering to zero above £100,000 income); **20%** basic rate to £50,270; **40%** higher rate to £125,140; **45%** above that ([The Private Office](https://www.theprivateoffice.com/tax-planning/uk-tax-allowances-and-brackets); [House of Commons Library](https://commonslibrary.parliament.uk/research-briefings/cbp-10618/)). Employee National Insurance is **8%** between the Primary Threshold (~£12,570/yr) and Upper Earnings Limit (~£50,270/yr), **2%** above it ([LITRG](https://www.litrg.org.uk/working/employment/national-insurance-employees)) — these bands are well-corroborated. The wrinkle specific to this occupation is that rope-access/offshore day-rate work is very often structured as self-employed or "inside IR35" umbrella contracting rather than direct PAYE employment, and one sourced umbrella-contractor example shows a £500/day, 220-day (£110,000) contract nets only about **£59,204/year** take-home after umbrella deductions, tax and NI — a reminder that headline day rates substantially overstate real net pay under this arrangement ([uktaxdrag.co.uk](https://uktaxdrag.co.uk/inside-ir35-contractor-money-guide-2026-27.html)).

**Norway**: flat **22%** tax on ordinary income after deductions ([countrytaxcalc.com](https://www.countrytaxcalc.com/tax-guides/norway-income-tax-guide-2026/)), plus trinnskatt (bracket tax) rising from 0% (below NOK 226,100) through 1.7%, 4%, 13.7%, 16.8% to 17.8% at the top ([taxatlas.io](https://taxatlas.io/country/norway/income-tax)), plus a national-insurance trygdeavgift charged on gross income. Two figures are **genuinely disputed between sources and unresolved in this round**: trygdeavgift is cited as both **7.6%** and **7.9%** with no reconciling primary source reached, and personfradrag (the personal allowance) is cited as both **NOK 108,550** and **NOK 114,540** — a discrepancy worth roughly NOK 6,000 in taxable income and material enough to shift a final net-pay figure by a percentage point or so. Minstefradrag, a separate general deduction of 46% of gross income capped at **NOK 104,450**, is well-corroborated and used below. A genuinely important structural finding buried in the tax note: free board/lodging on an offshore installation is normally a **taxable fringe benefit** in Norway, valued at standard daily rates (~NOK 151/day accommodation, ~NOK 107/day full board) that get added back into taxable income — the opposite of how free offshore accommodation might intuitively be assumed to work ([Skatteetaten](https://www.skatteetaten.no/en/business-and-organisation/employer/the-a-melding/the-a-melding-guide/salary-and-benefits/overview-of-salary-and-other-benefits/board-offshore-workers/)).

**Denmark**: a flat **8% AM-bidrag** (labour-market contribution) comes off gross pay before anything else, then **12.01% bundskat** (base state tax) plus municipal tax (kommuneskat) — nationally averaging **~25.05%** but running **26.1% in Esbjerg**, Denmark's main offshore-wind hub — apply above a **DKK 54,100** personfradrag ([The Local DK](https://www.thelocal.dk/20251113/middle-top-and-top-top-how-denmarks-tax-brackets-are-changing-in-2026); [Expat Finance](https://expatfinance.dk/taxes/municipality-tax-rates-2026/); [tvSyd](https://www.tvsyd.dk/esbjerg/esbjerg-haever-skatten-fra-naeste-ar)). A new three-tier top-tax structure took effect in January 2026 — 7.5% mellemskat above DKK 641,200, a further 7.5% topskat above DKK 777,900, and 5% top-topskat above DKK 2,592,700 — but none of it applies at the DKK 400,000-470,000 income range relevant to this project's offshore-wind pay estimate, since that range sits entirely below the first threshold. One figure could **not** be pinned down: the exact 2026 "skatteloft" (statutory tax ceiling that caps combined bundskat+mellemskat+topskat+top-topskat) was referenced by sources but its specific 2026 percentage was never retrieved — it does not affect the DKK 400-470k estimate below, but would matter for any higher-earning scenario.

**Netherlands**: combined wage-tax-plus-social-insurance (loonheffing) runs **35.75%** on Box 1 income up to **€78,426** for anyone below state-pension age, then **49.50%** above that ([Dutch Payroll Tax 2026 guide](https://dutchtaxcalculators.com/dutch-payroll-tax-loonheffing-2026-complete-guide-for-employees-employers/)); the 37.56% "middle bracket" some sources cite applies only to AOW-age filers and is not relevant to a working-age technician. The arbeidskorting (employment tax credit) is well-corroborated and phases from a maximum of **€5,685** down via a 6.51%-of-income taper above €45,592. Mandatory health insurance runs roughly **€1,884/year**, and the zorgtoeslag (healthcare allowance) cuts off entirely above **€40,857/year** for a single filer — meaning most of this occupation's pay range receives no such subsidy. A meaningful chunk of the Dutch rope-access/wind-technician market works as zzp (self-employed) rather than as an employee, which changes the calculation materially via the zelfstandigenaftrek (€2,470 fixed deduction) and MKB-winstvrijstelling (12.7% profit exemption applied after it) — these reliefs stack to lower a zzp'er's effective tax rate below an employee's on the same gross figure, though the zzp'er must separately fund pension, sick-pay insurance and holiday-pay buffers an employer would otherwise provide.

On this basis, **Norway's aggregate NOK ~515-522k gross converts to the highest net take-home rate of the four (~78%)**, but that gross figure itself was already flagged in the earlier report as likely a major understatement of true North Sea offshore day-rate income, so the comparison may be comparing Norway's low-confidence aggregate against the UK's better-corroborated oil-and-gas figure. The Dutch figures sit in between and would need the ~€1,884 mandatory health-insurance premium subtracted for a fully comparable "cash in pocket" number, which would bring both Dutch net figures down by roughly two points of take-home percentage.

## What a follow-up attempt needs to actually work

Three concrete changes would fix this round's failure mode rather than repeat it. First, **run the four country searches sequentially, one at a time, with deliberate spacing between calls**, instead of four parallel agents competing for one shared Indeed quota — the account-level rate limit is the single reason every country came back empty or near-empty, and parallelism made it categorically worse, not faster. Second, **supplement with a human or browser-enabled session that can reach the specific named real employers this round already identified**, since the network egress proxy blocked every career-site fallback tried here: for the UK, Oceaneering, NES Fircroft, Air Energi and Petroplan; for the Netherlands, Bluestream Offshore, Sky-Access, Rope Access Noord, Fender BV, OEG and Bilfinger's dedicated rope-access careers page; for Norway, DeepOcean, NOV, Intertek and Nordex — where the 9 real job IDs already found this round (Job IDs JOBSEARCH_100252 through 100264) just need a working `get_job_details` re-run, not a fresh search; and for Denmark, Vestas Esbjerg, Global Wind Service Fredericia and iPS Baltic. Third, **verify the disputed Norwegian and Danish tax figures against primary sources** — skatteetaten.no for the trygdeavgift (7.6% vs 7.9%) and personfradrag (NOK 108,550 vs 114,540) discrepancies, and skat.dk for Denmark's exact skatteloft percentage — once those sites are reachable, since none of this round's tax facts were cross-checked against government sources directly.

## Conclusion

This round's real lesson is infrastructural, not economic: a shared, account-wide API quota silently defeats any parallel multi-agent research design that assumes per-agent isolation, and no amount of patient retrying from a single session fixes a quota being drained elsewhere. The tax-facts salvage is genuinely usable — it lets the project's existing gross-pay estimates be converted into rough net figures for the first time — but it cannot substitute for the vacancy-level rigor the assignment asked for, and this report should not be mistaken for having delivered it. The next attempt is a solvable engineering problem (sequential calls, spaced retries, a working browser fallback) rather than a data-availability one: Norway alone already proved 9 real, relevant, currently-posted vacancies exist and are reachable by search — they just need their detail pages successfully pulled.
